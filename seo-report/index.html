SEO Report Template: What to Include in a Monthly SEO Report

Ryan Stewart
Founder, The Blueprint Training
Last updated: Nov 7, 2026
Read: 14 min

Your client doesn't read SEO reports.

They open it, look for one number, and close it. If that number isn't there, they start wondering what they're paying for.

An SEO report has one job: show the client that the work is turning into customers. Rankings and traffic are the evidence. They aren't the result.

What should an SEO report include? A monthly SEO report should show organic traffic, Search Console clicks and impressions, the conversions and calls that traffic produced, Google Business Profile and local map rankings, keyword rankings, AI search visibility, and the content and links you shipped. Lead with a one-paragraph summary of what changed and what you're doing about it.

Below is the exact SEO report we run for every client in Agency OS, one section at a time, with a screenshot of each.

Here's the short version:

  • Ten sections, in the order a client actually reads them.
  • Outcomes first: conversions and calls sit right under traffic, not at the bottom.
  • Local and AI search get their own sections. For most local businesses, the map pack drives more calls than the website.
  • Show the work: content published and links built, with the traffic each one earned.
  • One summary paragraph on top. Most clients never scroll past it.

What Is an SEO Report?

An SEO report is a monthly summary of how a website performs in organic search, and what that performance produced for the business. It pulls data from Google Analytics 4, Google Search Console, Google Business Profile, call tracking and a rank tracker into one place.

A good SEO report answers three questions:

  1. Are more people finding us?
  2. Are more of them becoming customers?
  3. What did you do this month, and what's next?

If your report can't answer all three in under a minute, it's a data dump. Not a report.

If you run more than SEO for a client, start with our guide to client reporting for agencies. It covers paid search, LSAs and the reporting tools. This post goes deep on the SEO report only.

What to Include in an SEO Report

Here are the ten sections, the question each one answers, and where the data comes from.

The order matters. Visibility, then outcomes, then the work.

Most agencies bury conversions on page twelve. Put them on page one, right under traffic, so the client sees the connection.

1. Organic Traffic

Organic traffic is the number of visits from unpaid Google search results, pulled from Google Analytics 4 (GA4). Show it month by month for at least the last two years.

One month means nothing. The trend is the story.

Show these three numbers:

  • Last full month, with month-over-month change.
  • Year-over-year change, so seasonality doesn't fool anyone.
  • The full history, so the client sees where you started.

Under the chart, add the organic landing pages: sessions, bounce rate, time on page and conversions for each page.

This table is where the real conversation happens. In the example above, a blog post about Social Security pay charts drove 149 sessions and zero conversions. The city practice-area page drove 175 sessions and five.

Read that again. Traffic isn't the goal. The pages that convert are the goal.

2. Search Console Performance

Google Search Console shows how a site appears in Google before anyone clicks: impressions, clicks, click-through rate and average position. Google defines impressions as "how many times your site appeared in Search results," according to Google's Search Console Help on the Performance report.

Traffic in GA4 is what happens after the click. Search Console is the visibility before it.

Report two views:

  • The last full week, with week-over-week change. Search Console runs two to three days behind, so leave the week in progress out.
  • The last 3 months vs. the 3 months before. This smooths out the noise and shows direction.

I'll be honest: the example above is a down quarter. Clicks fell 38.7% against the prior three months.

That's exactly why this section exists. A report that hides the bad months gets you fired when the client finds out on their own. Show it, then explain it and show the fix.

Top Search Queries: Branded vs. Non-Branded

List the queries driving clicks, split into branded (the business name) and non-branded (everything else).

Branded clicks mean people already know the client. Non-branded clicks are the new customers SEO is supposed to find.

Look at what's driving the clicks. In this example, informational blog topics lead the list while "medical malpractice lawyer" shows 4,099 impressions at position 11.6 and only 4 clicks.

That's your next month's priority: a money keyword sitting just off page one.

3. Organic Conversions

Organic conversions are the key actions completed by visitors from organic search: form fills, calls and bookings, tracked as key events in GA4. Google renamed conversions to "key events" in GA4, according to Google Analytics Help.

This is the section that keeps clients.

Show conversions by month, then break them down by event so the client sees what kind of lead it was.

In the example, organic visitors produced 22 first-time phone calls and 6 intake-form leads in the last 30 days.

If you don't have key events set up, fix that before your first report. No conversion tracking means you're reporting traffic and hoping.

4. Call Tracking

Call tracking assigns a tracking number to each marketing channel so every phone call can be tied back to its source. For service businesses, the phone is usually the biggest lead source.

GA4 can't see a call to the number on a Google Business Profile. Call tracking can.

Look at the source table in the example. Google Business Profile drove 451 calls in 30 days. Google organic drove 66.

That's nearly seven times more calls from the map listing than from the website. If this report only showed website traffic, the client would never see where most of their SEO calls come from.

5. Google Business Profile

Google Business Profile (GBP) performance shows how many people saw a business on Google Search and Maps, and how many called, clicked to the website or asked for directions. Google counts profile views as unique people, once per day, according to Google Business Profile Help.

For any business with a local service area, this section matters as much as organic traffic.

Report four numbers for the last full month:

  • Profile impressions, split by Search and Maps.
  • Website clicks from the profile.
  • Phone calls from the call button.
  • Direction requests.

In the example, the profile earned 2,636 impressions, 151 calls, 145 website clicks and 67 direction requests last month.

One caution: Google says the searches metric updates at the start of each month and can take up to five days to appear. Don't send the report on the 1st.

Local Rank Grids

A local rank grid scans a keyword from dozens of points across a city and shows where the business ranks in the map pack from each one. One rank number for "car accident lawyer" is meaningless in local search. Rankings change block by block.

Green means top 3. Orange means 4 to 10. Red means 11 to 20.

Each grid shows four numbers:

  • ARP (average rank position) across every point.
  • Top 3: how many points rank in the top three.
  • SoLV (share of local voice): the share of points where the business shows in the top three.
  • Found: the share of points where it shows at all.

In the example, the city personal injury keyword ranks top 3 on 28 of 49 points, with a 57.1% share of local voice. The grid shows exactly which side of town to work on next. The red column on the left is it.

We pull these scans from Local Falcon. For more on what moves those squares, see our breakdown of local SEO ranking factors.

6. Keyword Rankings

Keyword rankings show where a site ranks in Google for the search terms that matter to the business, with each keyword's monthly search volume and difficulty.

Don't track 500 keywords. Track the ones tied to revenue.

Show rank, change since last month, volume and keyword difficulty for each.

Volume is what makes rankings meaningful. Moving from position 3 to 1 on a 1,600-search keyword is worth more than ranking first for twenty keywords nobody searches.

Pick the target keywords during your keyword research, agree on them with the client, and report the same list every month.

7. AI Referral Traffic

AI referral traffic is the visits a website receives from AI assistants such as ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude and Copilot, tracked in GA4 by referral source.

It's small today for most sites. It's also the one number every client asks about right now.

In the example, AI assistants sent 293 sessions over 12 months, with ChatGPT the top source at 11 sessions in the last 30 days.

Report it monthly. The trend matters more than the total.

8. Generative Engine Visibility (AI Citations)

Generative engine visibility measures how often AI assistants name or cite a business inside their answers, even when nobody clicks. A citation reaches a prospect who never sees a traditional search result.

AI referral traffic only counts the clicks. Citations count the mentions.

The example shows 66 AI citations: 28 in Perplexity, 10 in Gemini, 2 in ChatGPT and none in Google's AI Overviews or AI Mode.

That gap is a strategy conversation. Zero AI Overview citations on a site that ranks on page one means the content isn't written to be quoted. We pull this data from Ahrefs Brand Radar, a paid add-on. If you don't have it, hide the section.

Brand Radar is one of several ways to measure this. Our guide to AI visibility tracking covers every option and how much to trust each one.

9. Content Published

The content section lists every page and article you published during the engagement, with the impressions and clicks each one earns.

This is how you prove the work.

The client sees what you shipped, when it went live, and whether it's gaining traction in search.

Sort by impressions. The pages climbing get internal links and updates. The pages falling get a refresh or a merge.

If you plan content from briefs, our content brief template shows how we decide what goes on each page before it's written.

10. Links Built

The link section shows how many backlinks you built to each page, and how those pages performed in Search Console over the last 90 days against the 90 days before.

Most link reports list URLs and domain ratings. The client can't do anything with that.

Tie every link to the page it points at, and to that page's clicks and impressions.

In the example, 4 links to a city practice-area page lined up with a 24% lift in clicks. That's a link report a client understands.

For how we pick which pages get links, see our link building strategy.

How to Write the SEO Report Summary

The summary is the only part most clients read. Write it last, and keep it to one short paragraph plus three bullets.

Here's the structure we use:

  1. The headline: leads and calls from organic and local this month, against last month and last year.
  2. What moved it: the one or two things that drove the change, good or bad.
  3. What's next: the priorities for next month, tied to the numbers above.
  4. What we need from you: approvals, access or intake fixes.

If the month was bad, say so in the first sentence. Clients forgive a bad month. They don't forgive finding out on their own.

How Often Should You Send an SEO Report?

Send a written SEO report monthly, and give the client a live dashboard they can open any day. SEO moves too slowly for weekly reports to mean much.

The quarterly review is where you reset strategy. The monthly report is where you show progress against it.

A live dashboard cuts the "how are we doing?" emails. In Agency OS, the same report lives in the client's private portal. See how to share a client report.

Hide the Sections That Don't Apply

Not every client needs every section. A national software company doesn't need local rank grids. A plumber may not need AI citations yet.

Turn sections off rather than show empty charts. An empty section looks like a failure.

SEO Report Mistakes

  • ❌ Leading with rankings. Lead with leads and calls.
  • ❌ Reporting traffic without conversions. Traffic isn't the goal.
  • ❌ Leaving out call tracking and GBP. For local businesses, that's where most SEO calls come from.
  • ❌ One rank number for a local keyword. Use a grid.
  • ❌ Hiding a bad month. Show it, explain it, show the fix.
  • ❌ No commentary. Charts without a sentence of context get skipped.
  • ❌ Tracking hundreds of keywords. Track the ones tied to revenue.
  • ❌ A link list with no results. Tie every link to the page it lifted.

SEO Report FAQs

What should be included in an SEO report? Organic traffic, Search Console clicks and impressions, organic conversions, call tracking, Google Business Profile and local rank grids, keyword rankings, AI referral traffic and citations, and the content and links you shipped. Put a one-paragraph summary on top.

How often should you send an SEO report? Monthly, with a live dashboard the client can check any day. Add a quarterly strategy review.

What's the most important metric in an SEO report? Leads: organic conversions plus calls from organic search and Google Business Profile. Traffic and rankings explain why leads changed.

Should an SEO report include local SEO? Yes, for any business that serves a local area. Google Business Profile calls and local rank grids often drive more leads than the website.

How do you report on AI search? Track AI referral traffic in GA4 by source, and track citations in AI answers with a tool such as Ahrefs Brand Radar. Report both monthly and watch the trend.

What's the difference between an SEO report and an SEO audit? An SEO audit diagnoses a site once, before or at the start of the work. An SEO report tracks results every month after. See how to run a website quality audit.
