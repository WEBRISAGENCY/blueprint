How to Land Bigger Clients for Your Agency

Ryan Stewart
Founder, The Blueprint Training
Published: Aug 13, 2021
Updated: Nov 10, 2026
Read: 10 min

Most agencies don't have a lead problem.

They have a client-size problem.

At $1,500 a month, you need 34 clients to hit $50,000 in monthly revenue. At $5,000 a month, you need 10. Same revenue. A third of the accounts, a third of the reports, a third of the calls.

How do you land bigger agency clients? Pick a niche where one new customer is worth a lot of money to the client. Price from that value instead of from what other agencies charge. Build proof a buyer can check, get in front of them before they're shopping, lead outreach with their own data, and sell like an advisor. Then deliver well enough that the next big client comes from a referral.

Here's how we did it at WEBRIS, where we only work with law firms, and how Blueprint members are doing it now.

Here's the short version:

  • Bigger clients come from the niche, not the pitch. A personal injury case is worth $15,000+ to a law firm. A pressure washing job is worth a few hundred dollars. Same SEO work.
  • Fix your pricing before your pipeline. If your floor is $1,500, a bigger client still pays you $1,500.
  • Proof beats promises. Real numbers, real reviews, a process they can see.
  • Most buyers aren't buying today. Be the agency they already know when they are.
  • Lead with their data, not your credentials. An audit finding gets a reply. A capabilities deck doesn't.
  • Deliver like the retainer is twice as big. That's where the referrals come from.

What Counts as a Bigger Agency Client?

A bigger agency client is one that pays a meaningfully higher retainer because the work is worth more to their business, not because they're a bigger company. For most SEO agencies, that means moving from $500 to $2,000 a month into $3,000 to $10,000 a month.

Where most of the market sits today: in an Ahrefs survey of SEO providers (published December 2023, updated August 2024), the most common monthly retainer was $501 to $1,000, and 68.8% charged $2,000 a month or less. Agencies averaged $3,209 a month.

So a $5,000 retainer isn't enterprise. It's just above average for an agency. The gap is in what you sell and who you sell it to.

Why Bigger Clients Are Easier to Serve

A $5,000 client usually takes about the same work as a $1,500 client. The audit is the same audit. The report is the same report. The monthly call is the same call.

What changes is the margin, and how the client behaves. A business that pays more has usually bought marketing before. It knows what it wants, and it judges you on results instead of tasks.

I'll be honest: bigger clients have real downsides. The sales cycle is longer, they expect more, and losing one hurts more.

The fix isn't dropping every small client tomorrow. Raise the floor for new clients and let the book turn over.

Step 1: Pick a Niche Where One Customer Is Worth a Lot

Bigger clients come from industries where one new customer is worth enough money to make a bigger retainer an easy decision. That's the whole trick. The retainer is a fraction of what you bring in, so the fraction can be bigger.

A new personal injury client is worth $15,000 or more to a law firm. A pressure washing job is a few hundred dollars. You can do identical SEO work for both, and one of them can afford $5,000 a month.

This is why we only sell to law firms at WEBRIS. And because law firm owners talk to each other, we've grown almost entirely on referrals inside one vertical.

When you're picking or checking a niche, ask three questions:

  • Do customers search before they buy? If they don't, SEO and paid search can't move the needle.
  • Is one customer worth thousands? Law, dental implants, HVAC replacement, roofing, elective medical.
  • Is there room to grow? Enough businesses that you never run out of prospects.

We break down the strongest options in 3 agency niches that generate millions in revenue, and how to own one in the complete guide to agency positioning.

Step 2: Fix Your Pricing Before Your Pipeline

A bigger client doesn't raise your price. You do. If your proposal template says $1,500, the law firm that could have paid $5,000 pays $1,500.

Set the number from three inputs:

  • Your floor. What it costs you to deliver, plus the margin the business needs. Never quoted, never shown.
  • The band. What agencies in your niche charge.
  • Their ceiling. What one new customer is worth to them.

Your price lives between the band and the ceiling. Not at your floor.

For paid search, we charge a flat fee and then a percentage of spend past a threshold. One account we just closed spends $50,000 a month across platforms. Our fee is $5,000 a month.

The full breakdown, including what WEBRIS charges today, is in 7 SEO pricing models for agencies.

Step 3: Build Proof a Buyer Can Check

Bigger clients buy on evidence: case studies with real numbers, reviews from businesses like theirs, and a process they can see before they sign. They've been burned by an agency before. They're looking for a reason to believe you're different.

Specifics win. A case study that shows a firm's calls per month before and after, with dates, beats "we helped a national brand grow."

Don't have big-name case studies yet? Use what you have. One detailed result in the niche you're targeting beats ten vague ones from everywhere.

The other proof is your process. Show prospects what they'll get in month one before they sign. That's why our proposals open with a real audit of their site, not a menu of services.

Step 4: Get Known Before They're Buying

Most of the businesses you want aren't shopping for an agency today, so the agency that wins is the one they already know when they start. The B2B Institute at LinkedIn calls this the 95-5 rule: 95% of your potential buyers aren't ready to buy right now.

Bigger clients don't Google "best SEO agency" and pick the top result. They ask peers, and they remember who taught them something.

For me, the biggest driver of large clients has been YouTube. Walking through real strategies on camera does the selling before the first call.

For WEBRIS, the other one is our podcast, Lawyer Up. Every episode is a conversation with the kind of firm owner we want to work with.

And ask for referrals. Most happy clients won't think to refer you unless you ask.

The full system is in how to generate predictable leads for your agency and 5 pieces of content that fill your agency's pipeline.

Step 5: Run Outbound That Leads With Their Data

Outbound to bigger clients works when the first message shows them something about their own business they didn't know. A capabilities deck gets deleted. A specific problem on their site gets a reply.

Inbound brings in whoever finds you. Outbound lets you choose exactly who you want to work with.

The finding has to be real and specific. "Your site has 14 pages competing for the same keyword" gets a reply. "We noticed some SEO opportunities" doesn't.

We pull ours from a website quality audit in Agency OS. It takes about 30 minutes and gives you a list of real issues on their site.

Send it to whoever owns marketing. At a smaller firm that's the owner. At a bigger one, it's usually a marketing director who vets agencies for the owner.

Step 6: Sell Like an Advisor

Bigger clients hire the agency that understood their business best on the first call, not the one with the best pitch. Buyers don't give you much time to show that. Gartner reported in September 2020 that B2B buyers spend only 17% of their buying time meeting with potential suppliers.

So use that time on their numbers, not your credentials.

Ask, then listen. The discovery call should be mostly them talking: what they've tried, what worked, what a new customer is worth, their close rate and who answers the phone.

Build the proposal from what they told you. Their site, their competitors, their market, their numbers. A $5,000 proposal that reads like a $1,500 template loses.

The Proposal Builder in Agency OS builds it from the call notes and the prospect's own data, so it's in front of them the next day. Every day between the call and the proposal gives a competitor time to get there first.

Give one recommendation. Not three packages. If they're not a fit, say so.

Some deals close on one call. Some take a year. Every week someone emails me: "Hey Ryan, we talked two years ago." Keep every no in a nurture list and keep following up.

The full process is in how to sell SEO without a sales rep and how to build an SEO proposal that closes.

Step 7: Deliver at the Higher Level

Landing a bigger client means nothing if you can't keep them, and keeping them is what brings in the next one. Higher retainers come with higher expectations, more reporting and less patience for missed deadlines.

Before you chase bigger accounts, check four things:

  • Your process is documented. Every deliverable has an owner and a due date. See the guide to SEO agency project management.
  • Your reporting shows outcomes. Calls, leads and customers, not just rankings. See client reporting for agencies.
  • You have room on the calendar. Bigger clients want more calls, at least at the start.
  • Your systems can carry the work. A team, or AI doing the busywork if you're running it solo.

Then it compounds. Great work gets a referral. The referral is a bigger client. You do great work again.

That loop is how agencies move upmarket without a sales team.

Mistakes That Keep Agencies Small

  • ❌ Selling to everyone. No niche means no proof, no referrals between clients and no reason to pay more.
  • ❌ Quoting from your costs. Your floor is the minimum, not the price.
  • ❌ Cheap wedge offers. We used to sell a $500 website quality audit, then upsell the work. Most people walked away.
  • ❌ Pitching before listening. The first call is for their numbers, not your credentials.
  • ❌ Template proposals. A bigger client can tell when the proposal isn't about them.
  • ❌ Chasing enterprise before your delivery is ready. One lost $10,000 client costs more than five lost small ones.
  • ✅ One niche, one offer, priced from their value, proven with their kind of results.

Bigger Agency Clients FAQs

How do I get bigger clients for my agency?

Pick a niche where one new customer is worth thousands to the client, price from that value, build case studies with real numbers in that niche, and get known through referrals, video and value-first outreach. Then sell on their data, not your credentials.

What's a good retainer for a bigger client?

For most SEO agencies, $3,000 to $10,000 a month. An Ahrefs survey published in December 2023 found agencies average $3,209 a month, while the most common retainer across all SEO providers was $501 to $1,000. Set yours from what a new customer is worth to the client, not from the average.

How long does it take to close a bigger client?

Longer than a small one. Some close on a single call. Others take months or years, which is why every prospect who says no should stay on a nurture list.

Should I drop my small clients?

Not all at once. Raise your minimum for new clients, and replace small accounts as they churn. That way revenue never dips while you move upmarket.
